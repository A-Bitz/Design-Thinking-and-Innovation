## 1. Design-Led Innovation

**Design-led innovation** puts user needs and experience at the centre of how an organisation creates new products, services and business models.

| Technology-led innovation | Design-led innovation |
|---|---|
| "We have a new technology. Who can use it?" | "Users have this need. What can solve it?" |
| Starts in the lab | Starts with people |
| Risk: great tech nobody wants | Risk: slower if research is weak |

### Case studies

| Organisation | What they did | Result |
|---|---|---|
| **IBM** | Trained thousands of employees in Enterprise Design Thinking and hired designers across product teams. | A Forrester study commissioned by IBM reported faster time to market and strong ROI. |
| **Airbnb** | Founders met hosts in person, noticed bad listing photos and took professional photos themselves. | Bookings rose; empathy became part of company culture. |
| **GE Healthcare** | Redesigned MRI rooms as adventure stories for children. | Far fewer children needed sedation; patient satisfaction rose. |
| **Bank of America — "Keep the Change"** | Observed that people rounded up payments; built an automatic round-up savings feature. | Brought in hundreds of thousands of new accounts in its first year. |
| **Indian Railways / IRCTC** | Simplified online and mobile booking for millions of first-time internet users. | Became one of India's busiest e-commerce platforms. |

## 2. Measuring and Evaluating Design-Led Innovation

ROI alone is not enough, because many benefits (trust, satisfaction, fewer errors) take time to show up in revenue.

### Quantitative metrics

| Metric | What it measures |
|---|---|
| **Net Promoter Score (NPS)** | Would users recommend it? |
| **Customer Satisfaction (CSAT)** | How satisfied are users? |
| **Task success rate** | % of users who finish a task |
| **Time on task** | How long a task takes |
| **Conversion / adoption rate** | How many people start using it |
| **Retention / churn** | Do users keep using it? |
| **Time to market** | How fast ideas reach users |
| **Cost savings** | Fewer support calls, less rework |

### Qualitative measures

- User stories and testimonials
- Changes in team culture and collaboration
- Quality of insights from research
- Number of experiments run and lessons learned

### A simple evaluation framework

```
 INPUTS            →   ACTIVITIES          →   OUTPUTS           →   OUTCOMES
 (people, time,        (research, ideation,    (prototypes, MVPs,    (adoption, NPS,
  training)             testing)                features)             revenue, impact)
```

## 3. Stakeholder Engagement

A **stakeholder** is anyone who affects, or is affected by, a project.

| Type | Description | Example (campus app project) |
|---|---|---|
| **Primary** | Directly use or are affected by the solution | Students, faculty |
| **Secondary** | Indirectly involved | Parents, IT staff |
| **Key** | Have strong power or influence | Principal, Dean, finance office |

### Stakeholder mapping steps

1. **Identify:** list everyone involved or affected.
2. **Analyse:** estimate each stakeholder's **power** and **interest**.
3. **Map:** place them on a power–interest grid.
4. **Plan:** choose an engagement strategy for each group.
5. **Engage and review:** keep updating the map as the project changes.

### Power–Interest Grid

```
        HIGH │   KEEP SATISFIED     │   MANAGE CLOSELY ⭐
             │   e.g. Finance office│   e.g. Dean, HOD
   POWER     ├──────────────────────┼──────────────────────
             │   MONITOR            │   KEEP INFORMED
        LOW  │   e.g. Vendors       │   e.g. Students, parents
             └──────────────────────┴──────────────────────
                  LOW         INTEREST          HIGH
```

### Influence of stakeholders: examples

| Case | Lesson |
|---|---|
| **Google Glass** | Ignoring the public (people being recorded) as stakeholders created privacy backlash. |
| **Aadhaar-based services** | Success depended on engaging government, banks, telecom operators and citizens together. |
| **Nokia's smartphone transition** | Internal stakeholders defended the existing platform, slowing the response to user change. |

> 💡 Stakeholders can make or break an innovation. Bring them in **early**, through *Playbacks* (IBM) and co-creation workshops.

## 4. Scaling Design Thinking Across Organisations

Going from one design-thinking team to the whole organisation needs more than workshops.

| Strategy | How |
|---|---|
| **Leadership sponsorship** | Senior leaders model and fund the approach |
| **Training at scale** | Online courses, badges, bootcamps (IBM trained hundreds of thousands) |
| **Embedded designers** | Designers inside every product team, not a separate silo |
| **Shared language and toolkits** | Common templates (empathy maps, Hills, Playbacks) |
| **Communities of practice** | Champions who coach others |
| **Measure and share wins** | Publish success stories and metrics |

### IBM's three practices at scale

- **Hills:** align teams on user outcomes (*Who + What + Wow*).
- **Playbacks:** keep stakeholders aligned through regular show-and-tell sessions.
- **Sponsor Users:** keep real users involved throughout the project.

## 5. Future Trends in Design Thinking

| Trend | What it means |
|---|---|
| **AI-assisted design** | AI helps analyse research, generate ideas and build prototypes faster. Human judgement and empathy remain essential. |
| **Sustainable / circular design** | Designing for the whole life cycle and the planet, linked to the UN SDGs. |
| **Inclusive and accessible design** | Designing with and for people of all abilities, languages and backgrounds. |
| **Data-informed design** | Combining qualitative insight with analytics. |
| **Systems thinking** | Looking at whole ecosystems, not single products. |
| **Ethical and responsible tech** | Considering privacy, bias and long-term harm, e.g. in AI and blockchain. |
| **Remote and digital collaboration** | Online whiteboards and distributed design sprints. |

## 6. Best Practices and Challenges

### ✅ Best practices

1. Start with a real, meaningful problem.
2. Involve real users throughout, not only at the start.
3. Build diverse, cross-functional teams.
4. Prototype early and test often.
5. Make work visible (walls, boards, playbacks).
6. Measure outcomes, not just activity.
7. Celebrate learning from failure.

### ⚠️ Common challenges and how to handle them

| Challenge | How to overcome it |
|---|---|
| Resistance to change | Start with a small pilot and share quick wins |
| "We don't have time for research" | Use lightweight, timeboxed methods |
| No access to real users | Use sponsor users, field visits, online communities |
| Design thinking treated as a one-off workshop | Embed it in everyday processes |
| Hard to measure value | Define metrics before starting |
| Siloed departments | Run cross-functional sprints and playbacks |

## 📝 Practice Questions

### Short answer (2–3 marks)

1. Define design-led innovation and explain how it differs from technology-led innovation.
2. What is a stakeholder map? List its typical components.
3. Distinguish between primary, secondary and key stakeholders with one example each.
4. Mention any four metrics used to measure design-led innovation.
5. What are Hills, Playbacks and Sponsor Users in IBM Enterprise Design Thinking?

### Long answer (8–10 marks)

1. With a suitable case study, explain how design thinking led to a successful innovation.
2. Discuss qualitative and quantitative methods for measuring design-led innovation. Why is ROI alone insufficient?
3. Explain the process of creating a stakeholder map. Illustrate it with a power–interest grid.
4. "Stakeholders can make or break an innovation." Critically examine this statement with examples.
5. Describe strategies to scale design thinking across an organisation, referring to IBM's approach.
6. Analyse the role of AI, sustainability and inclusive design in the future of design thinking.

### Case-based (10–15 marks)

**Campus innovation:** Your college plans to redesign its student grievance system using design thinking.
(a) Identify all stakeholders and build a stakeholder map.
(b) Classify them by power and interest.
(c) Propose an engagement strategy for each quadrant.
(d) Suggest three KPIs to evaluate the redesigned system after six months.
