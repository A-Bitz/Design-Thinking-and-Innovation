## PART A — Prototyping and MVP

## 1. What is a Prototype?

A **prototype** is an early, simple version of an idea that you can show, use or test. Its purpose is **learning**, not perfection.

> "If a picture is worth a thousand words, a prototype is worth a thousand meetings." — IDEO

**Why prototype?**

- Makes ideas **tangible**, so people can react to them.
- Exposes problems **early and cheaply**.
- Starts **conversations** with users and stakeholders.
- Lets you test **assumptions** before investing heavily.

## 2. Fidelity Levels

| | Low fidelity | Medium fidelity | High fidelity |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Looks like** | Paper sketches, sticky notes | Clickable wireframes | Near-final design and function |
| **Time / cost** | Minutes, almost free | Hours to days | Days to weeks |
| **Best for** | Exploring concepts | Testing flows | Testing details and usability |
| **Tools** | Paper, pen, cardboard | Figma, Balsamiq | Figma, coded apps |

> 💡 Start **low**. Increase fidelity only when the earlier version has taught you all it can.

## 3. Types of Prototypes

| Type | Description | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Paper prototype** | Hand-drawn screens | Sketch of an attendance app |
| **Storyboard** | Comic strip of the user's experience | 6 panels showing a student using a canteen pre-order |
| **Wireframe** | Digital layout without styling | Grey-box app screens |
| **Role-play** | Acting out a service | Students act as a help-desk and visitors |
| **Physical model** | Cardboard, clay, 3D print | Model of a smart dustbin |
| **Wizard of Oz** | The system looks automatic but a human runs it behind the scenes | A "chatbot" answered by a team member |
| **Landing page / video** | Describes a product that doesn't exist yet | Dropbox's explainer video |

## 4. Minimum Viable Product (MVP)

An **MVP** is the smallest version of a product that delivers value to early users **and** lets the team learn the most with the least effort (Eric Ries, *The Lean Startup*).

### Prototype vs MVP

| Prototype | MVP |
|---|---|
| Tests an **idea** or concept | Tests a **business** with real users |
| May not work at all | Must deliver real value |
| Used inside the team or with test users | Released to real early customers |

### The Build–Measure–Learn Loop

```
        ┌──────────┐
        │  IDEAS   │
        └────┬─────┘
             ▼
   ┌──────► BUILD ───────┐
   │                     ▼
 LEARN                PRODUCT
   ▲                     │
   │                     ▼
  DATA ◄──── MEASURE ◄───┘
```

The goal is to go round this loop **as fast as possible**.

### Famous MVPs

| Company | MVP |
|---|---|
| **Dropbox** | A 3-minute explainer video. The beta waiting list jumped from 5,000 to 75,000 overnight. |
| **Zappos** | The founder photographed shoes in local shops and bought them only when someone ordered online. |
| **Airbnb** | A simple website renting out air mattresses in the founders' own flat. |
| **Flipkart** | Started by selling only books online, delivered by the founders themselves. |

## PART B — Testing and Viability

## 5. Testing with Users

### Testing methods

| Method | What it tests |
|---|---|
| **Usability testing** | Can users complete tasks easily? |
| **A/B testing** | Which of two versions performs better? |
| **Think-aloud** | What do users think while using it? |
| **Concept testing** | Do users want this idea at all? |
| **Surveys after use** | How satisfied are users? |

### A 5-step user-testing process

1. **Plan:** decide what you want to learn and which tasks to test.
2. **Recruit:** find 5 real users (5 users reveal most usability problems).
3. **Run:** give tasks, stay quiet, observe and ask them to think aloud.
4. **Capture:** record quotes, errors and emotions.
5. **Iterate:** fix the biggest problems and test again.

> 💡 **Show, don't tell.** Don't explain your prototype. Let users struggle and learn from it.

### Feedback Capture Grid

```
 ┌──────────────────────┬──────────────────────┐
 │  👍 LIKES            │  🔧 CRITICISMS        │
 │  What worked well    │  What needs fixing   │
 ├──────────────────────┼──────────────────────┤
 │  ❓ QUESTIONS        │  💡 NEW IDEAS         │
 │  What confused users │  Ideas that came up  │
 └──────────────────────┴──────────────────────┘
```

## 6. Desirability, Feasibility, Viability

| Lens | Question | Evidence to collect |
|---|---|---|
| **Desirability** | Do people want it? | User tests, sign-ups, pre-orders |
| **Feasibility** | Can we build it? | Technical prototype, skills, cost |
| **Viability** | Can it sustain itself? | Pricing, revenue model, costs |

### Case studies

| Case | Outcome | Lesson |
|---|---|---|
| **Instagram** | Started as *Burbn*, a complex check-in app. Users only used photo sharing, so the team cut everything else. | Test, listen and **pivot**. |
| **Juicero** | A $400 Wi-Fi juicer. Reporters showed the juice packs could be squeezed by hand. | Feasible and fundable, but **not desirable** or viable. |
| **Google Glass** | Advanced technology, but privacy concerns and an unclear use case hurt it with consumers. | Technology alone is not enough; it later found a niche in enterprise use. |

## PART C — Pitching Your Idea

## 7. Structure of a Good Pitch

1. **Hook:** a story, surprising fact or question.
2. **Problem:** who suffers, and how much?
3. **Solution:** your idea, shown with the prototype.
4. **Evidence:** what users said during testing.
5. **How it works / business model:** how it creates and captures value.
6. **Team:** why you are the right people.
7. **Ask:** what you need (funding, mentoring, approval).

### Elevator pitch template (30–60 seconds)

> For **[target user]** who **[need / problem]**, our **[product name]** is a **[category]** that **[key benefit]**. Unlike **[alternative]**, we **[unique difference]**.

**Example:**
> For *first-year students* who *miss deadlines scattered across WhatsApp, email and the LMS*, **DueDay** is a *deadline-tracker app* that *pulls every assignment into one view*. Unlike *manual planners*, we *sync automatically with class announcements*.

### The 10-20-30 rule (Guy Kawasaki)

- **10** slides
- **20** minutes
- **30**-point minimum font size

### Communication tips

- Tell **stories**, not just facts.
- Show the **prototype** live or in photos.
- Use **one idea per slide**.
- Practise until you can do it without reading.
- Make eye contact and pause after key points.
- Prepare for the three questions you fear most.

## 8. Adapting to the Audience

| Audience | They care about | Emphasise |
|---|---|---|
| **Investors** | Return, market size, scale | Business model, traction, team |
| **Users / customers** | "What's in it for me?" | Benefits, ease of use, price |
| **Faculty / judges** | Process and rigour | Research, iterations, evidence |
| **Technical team** | How it works | Architecture, feasibility, risks |
| **Management / government** | Impact and risk | Cost, outcomes, compliance |

## 9. Putting It into Practice

Model creation and pitching close the design thinking loop:

```
Empathize → Define → Ideate → [ PROTOTYPE → TEST → PITCH ] → Implement
                                    ↑          │
                                    └── iterate┘
```

## 📝 Practice Questions

### Short answer (2 marks)

1. Define a prototype.
2. What is an MVP?
3. Differentiate low-fidelity and high-fidelity prototypes.
4. What is the 10-20-30 rule?
5. List the four quadrants of a feedback capture grid.

### Long answer (8–10 marks)

1. Explain the types of prototypes and fidelity levels with examples.
2. Explain the MVP concept and the Build–Measure–Learn loop with two real-world examples.
3. Describe the user-testing process. How do you capture and act on feedback?
4. Using a case study, explain desirability, feasibility and viability.
5. How should a pitch be structured and adapted for different audiences?
