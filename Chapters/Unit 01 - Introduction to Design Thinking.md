## 1. What is Design Thinking?

**Design thinking** is a human-centred way of solving problems. Instead of starting with technology or with what a business wants to sell, it starts with **people**: what they need, what frustrates them and how they actually behave.

It combines three lenses:

| Lens | Question it asks |
|---|---|
| **Desirability** | Do people actually want this? |
| **Feasibility** | Can we build it with the technology and skills we have? |
| **Viability** | Can it survive financially and keep going? |

An innovation is strongest where all three overlap.

> 💡 **Simple definition:** Design thinking is a repeatable process for understanding users, challenging assumptions, redefining problems and creating solutions that can be prototyped and tested.

---

## 2. Origins and Evolution

Design thinking grew over several decades:

| Period | Milestone |
|---|---|
| **1960s–70s** | The *design methods movement*: researchers tried to make design more systematic and teachable. |
| **1969** | Herbert Simon's *The Sciences of the Artificial* describes design as changing existing situations into preferred ones. |
| **1980s** | Design is described as a distinct way of thinking: solving problems by building and testing, not only analysing. |
| **1990s** | **IDEO** brings the approach into business, designing products around real user needs. |
| **2004–05** | The **Stanford d.school** formalises the five-stage model (Empathize → Define → Ideate → Prototype → Test). |
| **2010s–today** | Companies like **IBM** scale it to large teams (*Enterprise Design Thinking*). It spreads to healthcare, education, government and fintech. |

### Why organisations care today

- Users have more choices, so experience decides who wins.
- It **reduces risk**: bad ideas are caught early and cheaply.
- It builds **cross-functional collaboration** between design, tech and business.
- It gives a **repeatable process** for innovation instead of waiting for inspiration.

## 3. The Five Stages of Design Thinking

```
 ┌───────────┐   ┌─────────┐   ┌─────────┐   ┌───────────┐   ┌────────┐
 │ EMPATHIZE │ → │ DEFINE  │ → │ IDEATE  │ → │ PROTOTYPE │ → │  TEST  │
 └───────────┘   └─────────┘   └─────────┘   └───────────┘   └────────┘
       ↑                                                          │
       └──────────────── learn & iterate ←────────────────────────┘
```

| Stage | Goal | Typical tools |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Empathize** | Understand users' needs, feelings and context | Interviews, observation, empathy maps |
| **2. Define** | Frame a clear, user-centred problem | Point-of-view statements, "How Might We" questions |
| **3. Ideate** | Generate many possible solutions | Brainstorming, mind maps, SCAMPER, Crazy 8s |
| **4. Prototype** | Build quick, cheap versions of ideas | Paper sketches, wireframes, storyboards, role play |
| **5. Test** | Get feedback from real users and refine | Usability tests, feedback grids |

> ⚠️ The process is **not strictly linear**. Teams often jump back: a test may reveal a new insight that sends you back to *Define*.

### IBM's version: The Loop

IBM's **Enterprise Design Thinking** describes the same idea as a continuous loop:

**Observe → Reflect → Make → (repeat)**

It adds three practices for large teams:

- **Hills**: goal statements written as *Who* + *What* + *Wow*.
- **Playbacks**: regular sessions where the team shows its work and gets feedback.
- **Sponsor Users**: real users who join the team throughout the project.

## 4. Design Thinking vs Traditional Problem-Solving

| Traditional approach | Design thinking approach |
|---|---|
| Starts with the solution or technology | Starts with the user's need |
| Linear: plan → build → launch | Iterative: build → test → learn → repeat |
| Big launch, high risk | Small, cheap experiments, lower risk |
| Experts decide | Users and diverse teams co-create |
| Failure is avoided | Early failure is welcomed as learning |
| Mostly analysis | Analysis **plus** making and testing |

## 5. Divergent vs Convergent Thinking

Design thinking keeps switching between two modes of thinking.

```
   DIVERGE                 CONVERGE
  (create choices)        (make choices)
      ╱‾‾‾‾‾‾‾‾‾╲          ╱‾‾‾‾‾‾‾‾‾╲
     ╱           ╲        ╱           ╲
 ───<   explore   >──────<   decide    >───
     ╲           ╱        ╲           ╱
      ╲_________╱          ╲_________╱
```

| | Divergent thinking | Convergent thinking |
|---|---|---|
| **Goal** | Generate as many options as possible | Narrow down to the best option |
| **Mindset** | Open, curious, no judgement | Analytical, criteria-based |
| **Used in** | Empathize, Ideate | Define, selecting ideas, Test |
| **Example tools** | Brainstorming, mind mapping | Dot voting, impact–feasibility matrix |

> 💡 A good session **separates** the two. Mixing them ("that won't work!" during brainstorming) kills creativity.

## 6. Core Mindsets of a Design Thinker

1. **Empathy**: see the world through the user's eyes.
2. **Curiosity**: ask "why?" again and again.
3. **Bias towards action**: build something rather than only talking about it.
4. **Comfort with ambiguity**: it is fine not to know the answer yet.
5. **Collaboration**: diverse teams produce better ideas.
6. **Learning from failure**: fail early, fail cheap, learn fast.
7. **Show, don't tell**: use sketches, stories and prototypes.

---

## 7. Benefits and Limitations

### ✅ Benefits

- Keeps the focus on real user needs, so solutions are more likely to be adopted.
- Encourages quick, cheap experiments instead of one large risky launch.
- Catches wrong assumptions early through testing.
- Improves teamwork across disciplines.
- Builds a culture of continuous improvement.

### ⚠️ Limitations

- It can take more time and resources than building the obvious solution.
- It works best when you can actually reach real users.
- The non-linear process can feel unstructured to beginners.
- It can become a "sticky-note ritual" without real user insight.
- It is less suited to purely technical problems with fixed constraints.

## 8. Real-World Examples

| Organisation | What they did |
|---|---|
| **Apple** | Designed products around simplicity and user experience rather than technical specifications. |
| **Airbnb** | Founders visited hosts, saw that poor photos hurt bookings, and took professional photos themselves. Bookings rose sharply. |
| **GE Healthcare** | Children were scared of MRI scanners. The team redesigned the room as a "pirate adventure", and far fewer children needed sedation. |
| **IBM** | Trained thousands of employees in Enterprise Design Thinking to build products with users. |
| **UPI / BHIM (India)** | Simple, mobile-first payment flows made digital payments usable for millions of first-time users. |

## 9. Design Thinking in Blockchain & Quantum Computing

Design thinking is not only for designers. Some examples for your specialisations:

- **Blockchain:** Why do people find crypto wallets confusing? Empathy research can reveal fear of losing seed phrases, which can lead to better recovery flows.
- **Quantum computing:** Who will use a quantum cloud service? Researchers? Students? Designing the learning experience for first-time users is a design thinking problem.

## 🛠️ Activity: The Wallet Exercise (30 minutes)

Work in pairs.

1. **Design** (3 min): Sketch your "ideal wallet".
2. **Interview** (2 × 4 min): Ask your partner how they carry money, cards and IDs. Ask "why?" often.
3. **Dig deeper** (2 × 3 min): Look for feelings and frustrations, not features.
4. **Reframe** (3 min): Write: *"My partner needs a way to ___ because ___."*
5. **Sketch** (5 min): Draw 5 radical solutions for your partner.
6. **Share and get feedback** (2 × 3 min).
7. **Build** (5 min): Make a rough prototype with paper.

**Reflect:** How was your second design different from the first "ideal wallet"?
